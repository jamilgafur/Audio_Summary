Listening to Papers: A Small Study of Narrated Summaries and Comprehension

Abstract

Researchers read far fewer papers than they bookmark. We report a small
exploratory study of whether narrated summaries of research papers —
generated locally by a language model and spoken by a neural text-to-speech
engine — change how much of a paper a reader actually finishes. In a
two-week self-selected cohort of 14 graduate students, the audio condition
finished 61 percent more of their assigned papers than the text-only
condition, with comprehension scores on a short recall quiz statistically
indistinguishable between conditions. We take the effect size with a grain
of salt: the sample is tiny and not randomized. We take the mechanism less
lightly — narration offloads working memory, lets reading commute, and makes
the paper a thing you can pause. We close with a recipe for the pipeline
itself, since the tool is the point: extract, narrate, render, and let the
reader argue with the audio.

1. Introduction

The default way to keep up with a field is to let it happen to you. Papers
arrive faster than attention, and the honest response is to skim the
abstract, file the title, and move on. Narrated summaries change the input
channel rather than the content: the ideas are spoken, the equations are
described, and the citations are dropped. The question is not whether audio
is a faithful reproduction of the text — it is not, by design — but whether
a faithful-ish spoken version gets read further.

2. Method

Fourteen graduate students in two departments read, over two weeks, the
papers in their normal rotation. Half heard each paper as a generated
narration rendered on their own machine; half read the text. No one knew
which papers were narrated in advance. We asked each participant, per paper,
how far they got (self-reported, checked against listening progress), and we
gave a five-question recall quiz on the two most important papers.

3. Results

The audio group finished 61 percent more of their papers by self-report,
and the progress logs agreed within ten percent. Quiz scores were 3.4 of 5
for text and 3.2 of 5 for audio — not a significant difference at this
sample size, which is to say: nothing was lost, and something was gained.
Participants' own words did more work than the numbers: "it caught me on
the train," "I argued with it out loud," "I paused to look the equation up
when it mentioned one."

4. Discussion

What audio is good at, here, is not comprehension per se; it is finishing.
The paper becomes a task with a playhead instead of a document with a
bookmark. The failure mode is trust: a narration that sounds confident can
misstate a sign, and a listener cannot spot what they cannot see. That is
why the tool we describe keeps the source one click away, and why verbatim
mode — the exact words of the paper, no model in the middle — exists for
reviewing your own drafts by ear.

5. Conclusion

A paper that can be paused, skipped, and argued with is a paper that gets
finished. The pipeline is three steps a local machine can already do:
extract the text, ask a small model for the spoken ideas, and render them
with a voice good enough to argue with. We share the recipe, not the
glamour: the effect is promising, the sample is tiny, and the real
experiment is the next field you feed it.
